Screen businesses for sale on what's left after paying for everything the departing owner did for free.
How Muster works — read this first

Business ads quote profit earned by an owner who works in the business full time, usually unpaid. If you are not going to be that person, that profit is not what you would receive. Muster recalculates every listing on the assumption that somebody has to be paid to do the owner's job, then asks whether the asking price still makes sense.

Why "cost to replace the owner" looks so large

It is not one manager's wage. Most owners do three jobs at once, and the figure covers all of them: production or delivery (the billable work), management and admin, and sales and relationships. Open Details to see the hours, headcount and cost of each.

The rates scale with the size of the business — a corner shop is assumed to need a working supervisor, a $3m business a general manager. Where the ad does not say how many hours the owner works, Muster assumes 50 a week, which is typical but worth checking: it is often the single biggest assumption behind a Walk.

Who runs the business — and why it changes everything

The same business can be a good or a bad buy depending on who ends up running it. Muster prices three routes and the headline figures follow the best one actually open to you:

Keep existing manager
Cheapest by far, because their wage is already in the accounts — you only pay a premium to retain them. Only offered when the listing confirms someone below the owner already runs day-to-day operations.
Hire a manager
Always available, always the most expensive. A full market-rate salary to replace the owner.
Run it yourselves
Deliberately ruled out unless the role is genuinely light-touch (under 15 hrs/week) and needs no trade licence or industry credential. Otherwise you are buying a job in a trade you do not know.

Open Details on any listing to see all three side by side, each with its own return and verdict. A business can be a Walk if you hire and an Investigate if you keep the manager — that difference is often the whole deal.

What the colours mean

Go
Passes every test. Rare, and only possible once real accounts are verified.
Investigate
Passes on the numbers, but something needs checking or negotiating. This is your shortlist — worth contacting the broker.
Walk
Fails at least one test outright. One failure is enough — they are not averaged.
? Unknown
Not enough information to judge. This is not a bad score — it means the ad withheld something needed, usually the profit figure.

The priority score

The number under each verdict badge, priority 87, ranks how worth-a-closer-look the business is on its economics alone — return, multiple, payback and how far revenue could fall before it stops paying. It is scored out of 100 over whichever tests could be run, so a listing missing one input still gets a score rather than nothing. Sort by it to work down the list.

It is not a confidence measure. A high-priority listing built on unverified broker figures is still unverified — the verdict badge and the data-confidence row tell you that part.

The numbers

Where a benchmark applies it is spelled out in brackets, e.g. 12% (need 35%), so you always know which figure is this deal and which is the standard.

Annual revenue
Total sales for one year, before any costs come out. Not profit, and not a lifetime total.
Return
12% (need 35%) — this deal returns 12% a year on your money where a business this size should return 35%. Below the benchmark means you are being paid too little for the risk. A * means the benchmark was raised because the figures are unverified.
Earnings multiple
6.8x (max 3.5x) — the price equals 6.8 years of earnings once the owner's replacement is paid, where 3.5 years is the most you should sensibly pay. Brokers advertise a much lower number because they divide by the owner's earnings, which quietly assumes you work there unpaid. (Elsewhere this is called the effective multiple.)
Payback
Years to get all your money back. It is always a little longer than the earnings multiple — the multiple counts only the purchase price, while payback also counts the cash you need on top: working capital, legal and accounting fees, catch-up repairs. For a small business that extra is roughly a third again, so a 2.9x multiple shows as about 3.9 years' payback.
Max price
The most you could pay and still hit your target return. Compare it with the asking price — that gap is your negotiating room, or your reason to walk.

The "Fill gaps with industry averages" switch

Ads never state things like annual equipment upkeep, and most withhold profit. With this switch off, those listings honestly show — and sit as Unknown. With it on, Muster fills the gaps with typical figures for that industry so it can still produce a verdict.

Estimated figures appear in amber with a ~ mark, and so does anything calculated from them — if the profit was estimated, the return, multiple and payback built on it are marked too. and a listing running on an estimated profit figure carries a warning chip and can never be rated Go — a rough estimate is good enough to rule a business out cheaply, never good enough to rule one in.

How to use it

Turn on Fill gaps with industry averages and sort by verdict. Ignore everything marked Walk. The handful showing Caution are worth contacting the broker about — ask for three years of accounts, then enter the real figures and the verdict will sharpen. Nothing should reach Go on advertised numbers alone.

How the screen works. Earnings are reduced to what's actually left for an owner who isn't working in the business: profit pool (earnings less maintenance capex and systems uplift) minus management cost (the vendor's role priced at market rates, using the cheapest management route actually available to you) gives earnings after management, then a 10% transition haircut. Return, payback and price ÷ earnings are all measured against that figure, not against advertised profit — which is why a business marketed at 2.5× can still fail a 3.5× ceiling.

Gaps never move the score. A missing input shows as — with a "needs" chip and the affected verdict row reads insufficient data. Nothing is silently defaulted. Turning on benchmark estimates fills gaps with sector figures shown in amber like this — useful for triage, never counted in the verdict.
